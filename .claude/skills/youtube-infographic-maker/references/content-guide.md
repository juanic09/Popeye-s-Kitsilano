# Content guide — from transcript to infographic

The infographic is a study and action tool. Someone who never watches the video
should come away knowing what it teaches, why it's true (per the speaker), and
what to do on Monday morning. Someone who did watch it should find it a faithful,
faster-to-scan version of what they heard.

## 1. Extraction pass

While reading the full transcript, note every item that falls into one of these
buckets, with its timestamp:

- **Thesis** — what the whole video argues or teaches. Often stated in the first
  2 minutes and restated at the end.
- **Claims / insights** — statements the speaker presents as important or
  non-obvious ("the biggest mistake people make is…", "what most people don't
  realise…", "the key is…"). Signposting language is a strong signal.
- **Frameworks** — named methods, numbered steps, acronyms, models, formulas,
  decision rules, sequences.
- **Evidence** — stats, studies, results, stories and examples used to support a
  claim. Keep the best example per lesson; it's what makes a lesson stick.
- **Contrasts** — do vs. don't, myth vs. reality, beginners vs. experts, old way
  vs. new way.
- **Instructions** — anything the speaker tells the viewer to do, try, avoid,
  measure, or set up.
- **Quotable lines** — short, vivid, memorable.
- **Definitions** — terms the reader needs to understand the rest.

Skip: intros, sponsor reads, "like and subscribe", channel housekeeping,
tangents that don't connect back to the thesis, and repetition (keep the
clearest phrasing once).

## 2. Distil

**The Big Idea** — one sentence, ≤ 25 words, a claim rather than a topic.
"Procrastination isn't laziness — it's a short-term reward system winning
against a long-term one, and it's beaten by deadlines" beats "This video is
about procrastination."

**Key Takeaways (4–8)** — the things to remember. Each one:
- headline is a full claim in ≤ 10 words ("Deadlines are the only thing the
  Panic Monster responds to"), not a label ("Deadlines");
- body is 1–2 sentences: the reasoning or the evidence from the video;
- different from every other takeaway — if two overlap, merge them.

**Core Lessons** — the deeper teachings, in the order the video builds them.
Takeaways are *what* to remember; lessons explain *how it works* and include
the speaker's example plus "Why it matters". Typically 3–6. For short videos
with only a few ideas, you may fold lessons into the takeaways and drop this
section rather than repeat yourself.

**Framework** — reproduce the speaker's method faithfully: same step names,
same order, same count. Don't invent a framework the video didn't present; if
the video's advice is a loose list, it belongs in the Action Plan instead.

**Numbers** — only figures actually stated in the video, with what they
measure. Never round in a way that changes meaning; never add outside stats.

## 3. Action Plan — when and how

Include it whenever the video's content can be applied (skills, habits,
business, health, money, productivity, communication, learning…). Skip it, or
replace it with "Questions to reflect on", for purely informational content
(history, news, entertainment, science explainers) — forcing tasks onto those
feels fake.

Each action:
- starts with a verb and is specific enough to do without re-watching
  ("Write tomorrow's 3 priorities on paper before closing your laptop", not
  "Be more organised");
- has a `.how` line: the exact method, quantity, tool or what "done" looks like;
- traces back to something the video taught. Prefer the speaker's own
  instructions. If you convert a principle into a step yourself, keep it a
  direct, obvious application of that principle — don't add outside advice.

Group by timeframe: **Today** (≤ 15-minute starters), **This week**, **Ongoing
habit**. Rename phases if the video has its own structure (e.g. "Phase 1 –
Foundation", "Before the meeting / During / After"). 5–12 actions total is
typical.

## 4. Length — concrete, not long

Let the video's density decide, not its runtime alone:

| Video | Typical result |
|---|---|
| Short / Shorts, < 10 min, 1–3 ideas | 1 page |
| 10–30 min talk or tutorial | 1–2 pages |
| 30–90 min lecture / podcast | 2–4 pages |
| Multi-video set, 2+ hour courses | 3–6 pages |

If you're past these, cut before you shrink the font: merge overlapping points,
drop the weakest example, shorten every body line. Minimum body text 9pt.

## 5. Writing style

- Plain, punchy, active voice. Fragments are fine on cards.
- Use the speaker's own vocabulary for their concepts (names of models,
  characters, steps) so the reader recognises them.
- Bold at most one phrase per paragraph.
- No hedging filler ("it's important to note that…"), no marketing language,
  no emojis (they often don't render in print).
- Attribute contested or surprising claims to the speaker ("Urban argues…")
  rather than stating them as universal fact.
- If captions were auto-generated, sanity-check names and numbers that look
  garbled; if you can't resolve one, leave it out rather than guess.

## 6. Final self-check before rendering

- Could someone act on the Action Plan without watching the video?
- Does every section say something the others don't?
- Is anything on the page something the video *didn't* say?
- Is the Big Idea a sentence a person would actually repeat to a friend?
- Are all placeholders and unused components gone?
